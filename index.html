Master's thesis & practical project · 2026

Emotional visual storytelling in hotel booking

Two versions of the same hotel. Same rooms, same price, same booking flow. Only the pictures changed, and the difference in how people responded was measurable.

My role
End-to-end: research design, prototype, UI, testing, analysis
Method
Counterbalanced within-subjects, mixed methods
Participants
30 (15 A→B, 15 B→A)
Stimuli
14 matched scene pairs, mobile prototype
← All work
01 — The question

Does emotional imagery sell a room, or just decorate it?

Hotel-booking interfaces rely heavily on photography, but those images often function primarily as documentation. I wanted to test what changes when imagery also communicates an experience, and whether that shift affects the things a hotel cares about: intention to book, and trust.

The risk in asking that question is confounding. If Version B is warmer and better laid out and cheaper, the result tells you nothing. Interface structure, hotel information, price and interaction flow were held constant across both versions; the manipulated factor was the overall visual treatment: warmer lighting, contextual objects, atmospheric styling and selected human presence, applied together rather than one at a time.

02 — The two versions

Building the stimulus set

Fourteen scenes, each rendered twice. Matching them properly was the hardest design work in the project: the pair has to differ in register without differing in content.

Mobile prototype, approximately 402 × 874 px, with the full booking flow, an embedded Likert questionnaire, A/B randomisation and session export.

03 — How it was tested

Every participant saw both versions

A within-subjects design gives each person their own baseline, which matters when the thing you're measuring is subjective feeling. The trade-off is order effects: whichever version comes first colours the second. Counterbalancing handles it: half the participants went A→B, half went B→A.

  • Five-point Likert measures after each condition
  • Semi-structured interviews to get at the reasoning behind the ratings
  • A simulated booking choice at the end, after both versions had been seen
  • Interaction and behavioural logging throughout the session

The prototype combined the booking flow, questionnaire and behavioural logging in a single web-based study. Questionnaire and interaction data were transmitted through a Google Apps Script web application to a restricted spreadsheet and linked using pseudonymous participant identifiers, covering study and language version, presentation order, page durations, gallery-opening counts, ratings, the simulated booking choice and an optional open reflection.

04 — What the numbers said

Version B scored higher on every measure. Only some of it held up.

Version A — neutral Version B — enhanced Mean, 5-point scale
1.03.05.0

The three visual-emotional constructs moved decisively and survived correction for multiple comparisons. Booking intention moved in the right direction but did not stay significant. Trust barely moved at all.

The final choice was less ambiguous

After seeing both versions, participants picked one to book.

22 chose Version B — 73.3%
8 chose A

Exact binomial test, p = .016.

05 — What it means

Emotional pull and credibility are not the same signal

Core finding

A richer, more atmospheric interface can lift emotional response and visual appeal substantially without moving trust. Designing for feeling does not automatically buy you belief, and a booking product needs both.

The interviews explained the gap. Several participants described the enhanced version as more appealing and more staged: they enjoyed it while discounting it as advertising, and some rated the neutral presentation as more credible for exactly that reason. Realism itself was contested: for some it meant the absence of embellishment, for others it meant that human presence made the space feel alive.

People in the pictures divided the room

The qualitative data pushed back on a common assumption. Human presence in hotel imagery helped some participants project themselves into the scene, and actively repelled others. Participants who read a hotel stay as privacy, quiet or solitude experienced the same photograph as intrusion. There is no single correct answer here, which points toward segment-dependent imagery rather than a universal style.

If I ran it again

Trust was measured with three items: confidence, trustworthy, realistic (α = .85 for Version A, .91 for Version B). None moved significantly, but the interviews showed the construct was carrying more than one idea. I would use a broader validated trust measure that separates image credibility, provider credibility and confidence in the booking decision.

An academic study, conducted for research purposes. The prototype was not commissioned by, affiliated with or endorsed by any hotel or booking platform.

Team project · Hogrefe Innovation Lab · 2024

Dorsch Online — mobile usability evaluation

A psychology lexicon with thousands of cross-linked entries. On mobile, following a link means losing your place. We tested two ways to fix that.

My role
Contributed to planning, execution and synthesis as part of the evaluation team
Method
Comparative usability testing, think-aloud, observation
Participants
7, split into two counterbalanced groups
Setting
PFH usability lab, ~30 min per session
← All work
01 — The problem

How do you preview a linked term without leaving the page?

The Dorsch is dense, citable reference content: entries are full of linked terms that a reader needs to check in passing without abandoning the paragraph they're in. On desktop there's room to do that. On mobile there isn't, and every tap becomes a navigation event.

Two interface concepts were built in Figma and put head to head:

  • Tooltip: the preview appears in a compact overlay above the linked word.
  • Split-screen: the definition opens in the lower portion of the screen while the original content stays visible above it.
02 — Study design

Seven participants, two orders

Whichever concept a participant sees first sets their expectations for the second, so the sequence was alternated: Group A (4 participants) went homepage → tooltip → split-screen; Group B (3 participants) went homepage → split-screen → tooltip.

  • Warm-up interview on prior experience with reference tools and expectations
  • Homepage impressions, including what participants expected a linked word to do
  • Both prototypes on an Android device, with think-aloud throughout
  • Screen and audio recording, plus a separate observer taking structured notes
  • Post-test interview on preference, strengths and weaknesses
03 — Findings

Split-screen won on preference. Both had the same underlying flaw.

Tooltip

Preferred by 3 of 7 · satisfaction 7.5/10

Immediately understandable and easy to learn. Participants knew what had happened the moment it appeared. It broke down on long entries: too much text in too small a container, and reading while scrolling became genuinely difficult.

Split-screen

Preferred by 4 of 7 · satisfaction 8.2/10

Supported real comparison. Participants could hold the original sentence and the definition in view at once. The cost was space: the primary content got squeezed, and a few participants lost track of where they had been.

The shared failure

Across both concepts, clearer indication of the explained keyword and an obvious close control emerged as recurring usability needs, which made those the highest-value fixes, independent of which pattern shipped.

04 — Recommendations

What we handed back

For the tooltip: cap the preview length to around five lines where the content allows, reduce the overall footprint, add a visible close control, and mark the explained keyword much more clearly.

For split-screen: fix the panel height rather than letting it grow, strengthen the hierarchy between the two regions, show which word is being explained, add a close control, and improve the contextual cues that tell a reader where they are.

Across both patterns: copy support for citable text, audio support for the entry, and better onward discovery from a definition to related terms.

UX & product design project · 2025

MediConnect — healthcare search & booking

Finding a doctor in Germany as a newcomer is not a search problem. It's a filtering problem with the wrong filters.

My role
UX concept, persona, user flows, wireframes, mobile UI, prototype
Users
International students and newcomers with limited German
Output
Interactive Figma prototype
Focus
Product design thinking, end to end
← All work
01 — The problem

The information you need is never the information that's listed

Existing directories will tell you a practice's specialty and address. They rarely tell you the things that actually decide whether an appointment is possible: whether anyone there speaks a language you're fluent in, whether they are accepting new patients, and when they are actually available.

For international newcomers, practical information such as language support, new-patient status and availability can strongly affect whether a provider is actually usable. The pain points I designed against:

  • Language barriers at first contact, when the stakes are highest
  • No way to know in advance whether a provider takes new patients
  • Specialty names that assume you already know the German healthcare system
  • Insurance and service information scattered across sources
  • Booking processes that force a phone call as the only channel
02 — The journey

One flow, seven states

The flow prioritised practical decision criteria: language, location, specialty, availability and new-patient status, so that users could narrow the search before entering the appointment flow.

Home (location + language) → Results → Provider profile → Request → Calendar → Booking → Confirmation

03 — Design decisions

Making the decision criteria part of search

The design work went into making the criteria that decide an appointment visible as part of search rather than as details buried in a profile: language support, availability and new-patient status alongside location and specialty. The provider profile then carries the languages spoken, an about section, rating and practice location, and the request, calendar and confirmation screens complete the appointment path.

Honest limitation

This is a design concept, not a validated product. The flow was built from documented user problems and structured as a persona-driven journey, but it has not been usability tested. That's the obvious next step, and the one I'd argue for first.

Team project · User Interaction Analysis · January 2025

Blackboard Ultra — expert UX evaluation

An inspection-based evaluation of a learning platform students cannot opt out of, plus a controlled look at what an AI reviewer adds and where it goes wrong.

My role
Contributed to the expert evaluation as part of the project group
Method
Cognitive walkthrough, heuristic review, severity weighting
Scope
Three student use cases across login, materials and live sessions
Extra
AI-assisted review comparison; behavioural analytics study
← All work
01 — Approach

A repeatable chain from friction to fix

Expert reviews fail when they produce a list of opinions. We used a fixed chain so that every finding could be traced back to a task step and forward to a proposal:

Use case → task step → cognitive barrier → violated heuristic → severity (1–3) → redesign proposal

The evaluation was anchored to a persona, a full-time student who also works part-time, and to three ordinary scenarios: opening a document after an email notification, finding and downloading study materials before an exam, and joining an online class after a previous meeting overran.

The headline result was eleven findings: five things the platform does well, six that create friction. The positives matter for calibration: links behave predictably, documents open in the right format, viewer controls are intuitive, and the core tasks are completable. The platform isn't broken. It's slow in specific, fixable places.

02 — Findings

Where students actually lose time

Login

The institutional SSO option is buried inside a third-party dropdown and named in a way that doesn't match how students refer to it. The guidance next to it is in German on a platform used by an international cohort. There's no way to reveal a typed password. Fixes: lift the student login out of the dropdown, rename it in plain terms, provide English guidance, add a show-password control, and cut redirect steps.

Course materials

Download actions are hard to discover and only work file by file. Downloading multiple study files requires repeated individual actions, and nothing signals which documents are new. Fixes: cluster the download options under one consistent control, add folder-level download, show a document count outside the folder, and mark recently updated files.

Joining a live class

The path to an active session was not sufficiently visible from the course overview, and joining takes several steps from the dashboard, the exact situation in which a student is already late. Fixes: surface "Join session" directly in the course list, highlight the active course, and show session time and participant count.

03 — AI in the loop

We ran the same evaluation through an AI and compared

Each use case was passed to ChatGPT with the same inputs the human reviewers had: step description, cognitive barrier, violated heuristic. Its proposals were then clustered against ours.

Human review

More context-sensitive and more specific to the actual user. Better at solutions that require knowing why this particular student is stuck. Slower, and harder to scale.

AI review

Fast, broad, well-organised, good for a first pass and for standardisation-type fixes. Drifted toward generic suggestions when the prompt lacked context, and confidently proposed things that didn't apply.

Where I landed

The quality of AI output tracked the quality of the framing it was given, which means the expertise moves rather than disappears: into defining the use case, naming the barrier and judging what comes back. Useful as an accelerator, not as a reviewer.

The project also mapped how behavioural analytics tools (GA4, Hotjar, Amplitude and Heap.io) could support this kind of evaluation through funnel analysis, heatmaps, session recordings and A/B testing. This was an applied study of the tools, not an implementation on the live platform.

Qualitative group research · 2025

Humanisation of AI — helpful or uncanny?

What happens to trust and comfort when a system stops sounding like a tool and starts sounding like a person.

My role
Team member — qualitative research project
Method
Structured focus group, 7 participants, case studies and role-play
Themes
Trust, empathy, comfort, authenticity, the uncanny
Ethics
Written consent; anonymised documentation
← All work
01 — Design

Asking people to act rather than answer

To move beyond direct opinion questions, the guide combined open discussion with concrete cases and role-play scenarios: a short framing and welcome, a general discussion round, then two documented cases and two role-play scenarios in which participants interacted with the idea rather than commented on it.

  • Case one: a companion chatbot built to feel like a friend, and the point where support ends and emotional dependency begins.
  • Case two: AI teachers in South Korean classrooms, weighing lower pressure for anxious students, set against a perceived loss of warmth and spontaneity.
  • Role-play one: a conversation with an AI twin of a film director and an AI historian about a real event.
  • Role-play two: a student with an urgent question, an unavailable professor, and an AI twin of that professor offering to help.
Why role-play

The second scenario was designed to reduce the pressure to formulate an AI-friendly prompt and to encourage participants to interact with the system in a more natural, person-to-person style, which is exactly the behaviour the research question is about.

The session used a shared structured-observation template across all seven sections, covering seven participants and recording facial expression, body language, verbal emotional cues and engagement-related indicators. It also ran with written consent, anonymised documentation and a closing reflection round on the format itself.
